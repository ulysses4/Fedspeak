Educated guesswork · September 2026 FOMC projections

Dot Plot Detective

The Fed never says which dot belongs to which official. Based on everything each FOMC participant has said in public since the last meeting, here's an informed attempt to put names on the September 16, 2026 Summary of Economic Projections — and trace where each inferred voice sits through 2029.

How this was built, and how much to trust it

The Fed publishes only the count of participants at each rate level, never the names. Eighteen of nineteen participants submitted a dot (Chair Warsh opted out for a second straight meeting); one of those eighteen skipped the 2028–2029 columns.

This page ranks all 18 dot-submitters from most to least hawkish, using direct quotes and reported remarks gathered from Fed and regional-bank speech pages, wire coverage, and interviews between the September 16 hike and October 16 (the day before blackout). The rank is then mapped mechanically onto the real published counts at each level, for every column — the 4 most hawkish voices fill the 4 dots at 4.375% for 2026, and so on down the ranking.

That makes the chart internally consistent, not independently verified. The Fed has never confirmed a single name on this chart. Treat every placement as a labeled guess, strongest for 2026 (where there's the most public commentary to go on) and progressively softer further out — 2028–2029 in particular rest on thin evidence.

Dot color and the two hollow averages both use each column's actual voting roster for that year, not a fixed snapshot. The four rotating seats cycle through fixed groups of regional banks — Boston/Philadelphia/Richmond, Cleveland/Chicago, Atlanta/St. Louis/Dallas, and San Francisco/Kansas City/Minneapolis, one seat each — on a clock the Fed itself publishes. 2026's roster matches the Board's own "2026 Committee Members" list, and 2027–2029 match its "Federal Reserve Bank Rotation on the FOMC" table exactly (federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm). That part isn't a guess. Longer run isn't tied to any single year's committee, so no average is shown for it, and any dot in that column keeps 2026's color as a fallback label only.

Revised Oct 19, 2026: the first version of this ranking came almost entirely from this page's own reading of speeches. That missed things an independent cross-check against FedChirp (numeric hawk/dove scoring), Helious, MNI's Hawk-Dove Spectrum, and primary wire coverage caught. Four placements moved meaningfully: Bowman went from a guessed "leaning hawkish" (no in-window quote at all) to the single most dovish person on this page — MNI calls her "the former hawk turned clear dove," and she reportedly penciled in three 2026 cuts against the Committee's median of one. Jefferson moved the opposite direction, from "most dovish" to solidly hawkish-leaning: his widely-quoted "may take more time" line was about delaying a further hike, not resisting one, and the same speech called inflation risk "tilted to the upside." Kashkari and Paulson both moved up into the top hawkish tier — Kashkari dissented for a hike in July 2026 alongside Logan and Hammack, a vote that outweighs his later hedging on October's timing, and Paulson's single scored speech (+1.70) actually out-hawked Hammack's (+1.60). Goolsbee moved up from "2nd-most dovish" to the mildly-hawkish middle, per the correction described above. Every other placement held up under the cross-check.

On the 2027 gap specifically: this revision made it wider, not narrower. Because Kashkari and Paulson are now ranked among the top hawks and both rotate off the committee in 2027 too, the 2027 voter average (3.92%) now sits about 45 basis points below the non-voter average (4.375%) — up from 33bp in the first version. The underlying mechanism is independently noticed: Jack Fagan, CFA, at Osborne Partners wrote on October 4, 2026 that the incoming 2027 roster "looks less hawkish than 2026, but only modestly so," naming the same rotation (Logan and Hammack out, Goolsbee and Barkin in) with Atlanta's vacant seat as the wildcard — see "The Hawks Take Flight". His "modestly" and this chart's 45-basis-point gap disagree even more now than before. Treat the direction of the gap as corroborated by independent reporting, the size of it as this chart's own construction — and a construction that got more extreme, not less, the harder it was checked.

Known limits of this reconstruction:
  • Powell left no public record at all in this window; independent trackers split evenly on him too, so his placement is a genuine coin-flip between neutral and mildly dovish. Bowman had no in-window quote either, but unlike Powell, independent reporting (MNI, FedChirp) gives a clear, confirmed dovish read on her 2026 stance, so her placement rests on that rather than a guess.
  • The 2028–2029 skip is attributed here to Atlanta's interim president Cheryl Venable, a plausible but unconfirmed guess — the Fed doesn't say who sat those out.
  • Voter/non-voter coloring and the two averages follow each year's actual rotating roster, confirmed against the Fed's own 2026 membership list and its published 2027–2029 rotation table — that part of the chart is fact, not inference. The Longer Run column has no roster of its own, so it isn't colored by rotation.
  • This is a model of relative hawkishness, not a leak. If the real roster ever surfaces, expect several names to move.
